01

The case study

A wider role.
A human presence.

Understanding the responsibilities and skills behind the uniform.

Hear from a guard ↓

For passengers, a train guard may be the person who checks tickets, makes announcements or offers help. A serving, safety-critical trained guard’s account reveals a wider role: responding to emergencies, identifying hazards and supporting passengers when journeys become difficult.

The challenge

A railway journey can change quickly. A train may develop a fault, a passenger may need urgent assistance, or disruption may leave people unsure how to reach their destination. Serious incidents create additional challenges, particularly where passengers are close to moving trains or electrified infrastructure.

The guard’s account highlights the value of having a trained member of staff on board whose responsibilities include passenger safety and support.

Responding when something goes wrong

The author describes responsibilities that can include helping to protect a train following a serious incident, raising the alarm and supporting evacuation. The account also identifies situations such as an incapacitated driver, a train becoming divided or a passenger door opening during a journey.

These examples show why the role requires training, judgement and clear communication. An emergency response involves coordinating with other railway staff as well as helping passengers.

Maintaining safety during everyday journeys

The guard’s work also involves preventing incidents. The author highlights safe dispatch, including attention to the risk of passengers becoming trapped in doors and dragged as a train moves.

Reporting faults, identifying infrastructure hazards and responding to antisocial behaviour are further parts of the account. Where procedures permit, taking an affected coach out of use can help manage a fault while allowing the rest of the train to remain in service.

Supporting passengers through disruption

After a distressing incident, passengers may need information and reassurance while the driver and other railway staff deal with the operational response.

The author also describes assisting people in distress and raising the alarm when further help is needed. This aspect of the role depends on observation, calm communication and recognising when to seek support.

Helping make journeys accessible

The account places particular emphasis on disabled passengers and people who need additional assistance.

A broken lift, an inaccessible station or an unexpected change can turn an ordinary journey into a difficult one. The guard describes identifying these barriers, reporting problems and helping passengers access assistance or alternative arrangements.

The competencies behind the role

Across these responsibilities, the account illustrates five core competencies:

  • Situational awarenessnoticing faults, hazards and passengers who need help.
  • Safety knowledgeunderstanding and applying relevant rules and procedures.
  • Judgement under pressureassessing situations and prioritising action.
  • Communication and coordinationsharing clear information with passengers and railway colleagues.
  • Passenger careresponding to distress, disability and individual needs with patience and respect.

The central lesson

This practitioner’s account makes visible work that passengers may rarely notice when a journey runs smoothly. The guard’s contribution lies both in routine prevention and in being ready to respond when circumstances change.

For the author, the case for retaining safety-critical guards rests on that combination: trained operational support and a human presence for passengers.

02

The testimony

The role of
a train guard.

A practitioner account, credited to Active Train Guard.

“

Let the drivers drive and the Guards look after passengers.

Active Train Guard

The main role of the Train Guard in an emergency is to secure the train they’re working and to stop other trains ploughing into the train that’s either broken down, derailed or crashed. This role also incorporates making sure the various safety rules set out in the railways rule book are adhered to.

  • Locking out coaches for various safety reasons (e.g. no lights, failed passenger communications system, no air-conditioning or heating in extreme weather conditions, broken windows, trains too long for stations, failed fire systems) can help prevent cancellations by only locking out the coach affected and not more vehicles than is needed (which is the case without a guard).
  • Conduct a planned/controlled or unplanned/uncontrolled train evacuation whilst alerting the correct signalling centre, stopping other train services from entering the affected area. Plus contacting the electrical supplier to switch off overhead lines or third rail (AC or DC). Direct passengers to a point of safety and away from electrified lines or tunnels.
  • Taking the necessary steps to secure a train if: 1) driver is incapacitated, 2) train divides or passenger door opens whilst in service.
  • Look after the passengers whilst a driver is being replaced due to an incident (e.g. a fatality, potential signal passed at danger), vandalism like bricks thrown at trains, near misses, train hitting farm-yard equipment.
  • Helping with the unusual movements of trains when the signalling system fails or planned and unplanned engineering works require trains run in a different way.
  • Help talk down or monitor suicidal passengers and trespassers on the network.
  • Some trains require brake tests, checks for flat tyres, hot axles which need a second person to confirm or speed the check up.
  • Able to raise the alarm of any rail infrastructure issue on and off track that could endanger passengers and alert the correct authority.

Everyday work and the case for guards

When the worst doesn’t happen, the day-to-day role is to dispatch trains in a safe manner which helps prevent accidents such as trap & drag (where a passenger gets trapped in the doors potentially dragging them under, which could disable them or lead to a fatality. To assist in the prevention of anti-social behaviour onboard trains and at frequented stations and then raise the alarm if required. Conduct train safety revenue checks, report faults with trains and other railway infrastructure.

Too often on Driver Only services the overlooked passengers are the ones with disabilities or those that need reassurance/assistance.  When a Train has a properly trained safety critical Guard onboard they can turn up and go, with no booking or appointment needed just like every other passenger, only with a little extra care; for example, when the lifts don’t work, lights are out at stations, or the trains are too long for stations on the network we can lock out the coach, or by reporting issues early we can help to sort out alternative transport, or force a reluctant train operator to call an 24 hour maintenance firm to come out and solve the issue. Train managers are there from start of a train service to the very last train on railway services, ensuring freedom to travel and compliance with disability laws. We are the only guarantee of staff during the 20 hours of the day when trains are running.

Driver Only Operation is a 1980s era argument. Governments come and go but unfortunately arguments repeated by the same old railway management are devoid of any new ideas. They are hell bent on wanting to cut corners by de-staffing Trains & Stations. The railways always sell the dream of incorporating new technology but never see it fully though or stabilise the currently available technology. Too often, train operators will ignore practical railway issues by blaming the unions and staff. No matter how much technology advances it will take many years to get full 100% mobile & GPS coverage on the railways due to cuttings, tunnels, viaducts and the many isolated rural areas in Britain.

The questions that needs to be asked are, can 200 plus seater planes fly without a co-pilot? Maybe? But would it be safer? NO! Are late night buses safer since conductors were removed? NO! Have fares been reduced on the existing Driver Only services? NO!

TWO people are always better than one, no matter what the safety regulators that are paid for by the same people that want to implement these schemes tell you. Let the drivers drive and the Guards look after passengers.

Active Train Guard
